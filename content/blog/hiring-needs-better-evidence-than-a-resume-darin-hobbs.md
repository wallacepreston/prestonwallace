A job title tells you very little about what someone can do. A candidate might have the right experience and still struggle to describe it in the language an employer uses.

[Darin Hobbs](https://www.linkedin.com/in/darinhobbs/) has spent years working on that problem. At Western Governors University, he helped develop its digital achievement wallet. Through his consulting firm, [Covered Bridge Collective](https://coveredbridgecollective.com/), he works with iQ4 on Learning and Employment Records and credential wallets.

His vision gives employers a way to find people through evidence of their skills, while giving individuals control over how that evidence is shared.

## Help employers find the skills they need

Darin sees an opportunity to change how employers find talent. An employer could describe the skills a role needs, find people whose records show those skills, and review their experience before deciding whom to interview.

A Learning and Employment Record, or LER, brings together information about a person's learning and work. Digital credentials can connect achievements from college, workforce training, certifications, or military experience to the skills they represent.

That gives employers more to work with when looking for a match. It also helps people recognize and explain skills they may have trouble naming themselves.

For Darin, an important part of this vision is that someone can choose to be discoverable while already employed. They can decide how to present their experience and consider offers from employers looking for those skills.

As a side note, a related project of mine, [LLM Contact Discovery](/portfolio/llm-contact-discovery/), came up in our conversation: It searches university websites and brings contact records, source links, and review controls into one place. It shows how a clear search can lead to a list someone can inspect. Darin's vision takes that idea into skills-rich records that individuals choose to share.

## Make the job description specific enough to match

Darin's point about job descriptions is easy to overlook: the employer has work to do too.

A matching system needs a clear account of what the job involves. Broad phrases and familiar titles leave too much room for interpretation. Employers need to name the skills the person will actually use.

For example, a role involving data analysis could require cleaning survey responses, building reports, or explaining findings to customers. Those details make it easier to connect the work to someone's experience. This is an illustration of the kind of detail Darin is calling for.

The flow he described looks like this:

This shows Darin's proposed hiring approach. The matching step helps an employer find people worth talking with. The employer still reviews their experience and decides whom to interview.

## Give people control of their credentials

Darin places individual ownership at the center of a credential wallet. A person should be able to collect credentials from different issuers, keep them together, and choose who can see them. Each credential should retain a path back to its issuer so it can be checked.

That ownership matters as someone changes jobs, takes courses, or earns certifications through different providers. Their record needs to travel with them.

WGU's [Achievement Wallet](https://www.wgu.edu/achievement-wallet.html) offers a concrete example. It connects coursework and experience to skills, helps learners explore career paths, and lets them share a full or tailored wallet with employers.

Darin also described time-limited sharing as a direction the ecosystem is moving toward. He sees connecting wallets with employers' hiring systems as another important step in making these records useful at a larger scale.

## Use AI to explain skills and the next step

Darin described iQ4 using AI to work with detailed skills information. Context matters here: a skill label becomes more useful when it explains what someone can do and where that ability applies.

He also sees potential for an agent that helps people plan what to learn next. It could explain which skills are gaining demand in their area, which need refreshing, and which credentials teach the skills they want to build. Comparing course costs could help them choose a practical path.

That is a future capability he would like to see. The useful idea is advice connected to a person's existing skills and the work available to them.

Darin's approach gives hiring teams a practical place to start: take one role and spell out the skills used in its day-to-day work. That clarity makes it easier to recognize relevant credentials, ask useful interview questions, and help people see where their experience fits.
