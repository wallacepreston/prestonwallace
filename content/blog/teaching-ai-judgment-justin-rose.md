A student can produce a polished answer with AI without understanding the subject. The assignment may look complete, but the learning that should have happened along the way is harder to see.

I spoke with [Dr. Justin Rose](https://www.linkedin.com/in/justinbrose/), director of AI strategy and institutional innovation at [Pitt Community College](https://pittcc.edu/about-pcc/planning-and-research/pr-contacts/), about how educators can respond. Rose also teaches business courses and supports AI practice across North Carolina's community colleges. His approach puts student judgment into the work itself.

## Ask students to think before using AI

Rose described a practice he uses with his children: before opening the computer, they talk through what they want to make and organize the details. The exercise gives them a reason to form an idea before asking a tool to produce one.

The same principle can guide an assignment. Ask students to identify the problem, make an initial decision, or explain their reasoning before they use AI. That first step gives them something to compare with the output and a reason to notice when the answer misses the point.

## Design assignments that make thinking visible

Rose described plans for three-minute video discussion posts in his upcoming classes. Students would explain the topic aloud, watch a peer's contribution, and then respond. His goal is to make students engage with the material and each other.

A video is one possible format. The useful design question is what evidence of understanding the assignment asks for. An explanation, reflection, or response to a peer can give an instructor more to discuss with a student than a finished answer alone. Each format still needs to fit the course and its students.

## Make AI errors part of the lesson

Rose also described assignments that require students to interact with AI, identify errors, and answer questions about what went wrong. He wants students to encounter the limits of the tool as they build fluency with it. In his courses, the quality of their response to a mistake matters.

That creates a useful exercise for any discipline: give students an output they can check against what they are learning, then ask them to explain the evidence behind their corrections. A student has to understand enough of the subject to challenge a confident answer.

Rose calls his broader teaching approach [G3](https://g3.lpsnc.com/), for guided, grounded, and graduated learning. He described the goal as developing subject knowledge alongside AI fluency. The framework gives instructors a way to increase student independence as they practice.

## Put boundaries around the work

Rose uses a practical privacy rule he calls the telemarketer test: if you would not give information to a telemarketer, pause before putting it into an AI tool. He also asks people to consider what a tool can access and whether their information may be used for training.

For an educator, that means deciding which information is appropriate for an exercise and checking the institution's approved tools and policies. The exact settings and data protections depend on the service and agreement in use.

## Teach the subject and the judgment together

Rose compared learning AI to learning to use a hammer. A beginner can bend nails even though the tool looks simple. Getting better takes practice and attention to the mistakes.

His point applies to assignment design: give students a reason to make a judgment, check the result, and explain what they would change. The tool can become part of the learning process when the student remains responsible for the work.
